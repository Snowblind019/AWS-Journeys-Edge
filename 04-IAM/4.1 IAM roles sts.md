## What I built

- **`LabAssumeRole`** — a role carrying two separate policies: a **permissions policy** (`AmazonS3ReadOnlyAccess`, what the role can do) and a **trust policy** (who is allowed to become it).
- **`lab-user`** — an IAM user with its own CLI profile and, to start, zero attached permissions. The principal doing the assuming.
- Assumed the role via `sts:AssumeRole`, exported the returned temporary credentials, and confirmed the caller identity changed from `:user/lab-user` to an **assumed-role session ARN**, then used the role's S3 read permission.

The whole point was to make the two-policy structure of a role concrete: permissions policy and trust policy are independent, and the trust policy is the gatekeeper for assumption.

---

## The evaluation model this proves

Role assumption is the exception to normal same-account policy logic. For most resources, an explicit `Allow` in **either** an identity-based policy **or** a resource-based policy is enough. Role trust policies (and KMS key policies) do not work that way: the trust policy is the role's resource-based policy, and it is **mandatory** for assumption. An identity policy granting `sts:AssumeRole` is never sufficient on its own if the trust policy doesn't permit the principal.

Same-account, the decision then splits on *how* the trust policy names the principal:

| Trust policy `Principal` | Identity policy needed? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The user ARN directly (`:user/lab-user`) | No | A same-account resource-based grant to a named IAM user ARN is sufficient by itself. |
| The account root (`:root`) | Yes | `:root` grants to no specific principal; it delegates the decision to the account's identity policies, so the user needs `sts:AssumeRole`. |
| A different principal (not you) | N/A — denied | Identity permissions cannot substitute for the trust policy. |

---

## What each demo proved

**Demo A — trust names the user directly, no identity policy → works.**
`lab-user` has nothing attached and still assumes the role, because the trust policy is a resource-based grant naming the user's ARN. Same-account, that grant stands on its own. The S3 read came entirely from the role's permissions policy, not the user.

**Demo B — trust trusts `:root`, no identity policy → fails; add identity policy → works.**
Switching the principal to `arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root` removes the direct grant. `:root` means "defer to this account's identity policies," so with no `sts:AssumeRole` on the user, the call is `AccessDenied`. Attaching an identity policy that allows `sts:AssumeRole` on the role makes it succeed. This is the source of both classic confusions at once: *"I have AssumeRole permission but still get AccessDenied"* (trust doesn't name me) and *"I have no permissions but can still assume"* (trust names me directly).

**Demo C — full `AdministratorAccess`, trust names someone else → fails.**
`AccessDenied` despite admin on the identity side. The trust policy lists a different principal, and identity permissions never punch through a trust policy that doesn't allow you. This is the trust-policy exception in action.

## STS / session mechanics

- `AssumeRole` returns temporary credentials: `AccessKeyId`, `SecretAccessKey`, `SessionToken`, and an `Expiration`.
- Default session length is **1 hour**; the ceiling is the role's `MaxSessionDuration` (1–12h). **Role chaining** (assuming a role from an already-assumed session) caps the session at **1 hour** regardless of `MaxSessionDuration`.
- After assuming, the principal for every subsequent request is the **session ARN** — `arn:aws:sts::111122223333:assumed-role/LabAssumeRole/<session-name>` — not the role ARN. This matters for any policy that conditions on the principal.

---

## Permissions boundary interaction (the subtle one)

A permissions boundary is a ceiling on an entity's **identity-based** permissions. It does **not** uniformly cap everything the entity can do.

Same-account, a resource-based policy that grants to a **directly named IAM user ARN** is **not limited by an implicit deny in a permissions boundary**. So in the Demo A setup — trust policy names `lab-user` directly — attaching a permissions boundary to `lab-user` that allows only `s3:*` (and therefore implicitly denies `sts:AssumeRole`) does **not** block the assume. The resource-based grant stands.

The boundary only bites when:
- the assume depends on an **identity-based** grant (the `:root` / Demo B path), where the boundary's implicit deny does cap the user; or
- the boundary contains an **explicit `Deny`** on `sts:AssumeRole` — an explicit deny always wins, even against a directly-named resource-based grant.

Decision rule: *implicit* deny in a boundary cannot override a same-account resource-based grant to a named user ARN; an *explicit* deny can.

---

## Exam relevance

- A broken assume is usually fixed by **adding the principal to the trust policy**, not by attaching another identity policy. Watch for that as the "more correct" option.
- **Trust-policy-first debugging:** "has `sts:AssumeRole` but `AccessDenied`" → the trust policy doesn't name them.
- **Temporary credentials** (short-lived, rotating, session-scoped) are the secure-by-default answer for applications, cross-account, and federation, versus long-lived IAM user access keys.
- The **boundary subtlety** above is exactly the kind of granular distinction that separates two otherwise-correct answers: a resource-based grant to a named user ARN bypasses the boundary's implicit deny in the same account.
