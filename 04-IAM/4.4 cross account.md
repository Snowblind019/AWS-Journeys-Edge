# 4.4 — Cross-Account Access & the Confused Deputy

**Domain 4: Identity and Access Management (20%)**
**Companion to:** `scs-c03-lab-4_4-cross-account-confused-deputy.md` (the runnable lab). This file records what was built and *why each result happens*, one level deeper than the lab's terse `Result:` lines.

**Cost:** $0. STS, IAM, and Organizations calls are free. Reuses the org and member account from 4.3 as the two accounts, so no new residue, the trust-policy and user-policy edits revert cleanly.

---

## What I built

- **Account A** (the caller) holding `xacct-user`, and **Account B** (the resource owner) holding `CrossAccountReadRole`, whose trust policy names `xacct-user`.
- Assumed `CrossAccountReadRole` across the account boundary, then layered two condition keys onto B's trust policy in turn; `sts:ExternalId` and `aws:PrincipalOrgID`, watching each gate an already-allowed assume.

The point was to take the exact trust-plus-identity machinery from 4.1 and run it across an account line, where the same-account shortcut disappears and the trust policy becomes the place condition keys get bolted on.

---

## The evaluation model this proves

Cross-account assume is the 4.2 rule made physical: **same-account is a union, cross-account is an intersection.** Same-account (4.1), a resource-based grant naming the principal directly is sufficient on its own. Cross-account, that shortcut is gone, both the trusting account's resource policy (the trust policy) **and** the calling account's identity policy must allow.

| Setup | Trust policy (Account B) | Identity policy (Account A) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same-account (4.1) | names the user directly | none | Works — direct resource grant is sufficient |
| Cross-account | names the user directly | none | `AccessDenied` — both sides required |
| Cross-account | names the user directly | grants `sts:AssumeRole` on the role | Works |

And the trust policy *allowing* the assume is not the end of it. Condition keys (`ExternalId`, `PrincipalOrgID`) gate a call that identity and trust already permit — a third lock layered on top of the two-lock model, not a replacement for either.

---

## What each demo proved

**Demo A — cross-account needs both sides.**
B's trust names `xacct-user` directly and the user has no identity policy → `AccessDenied`. This is the identical setup that *worked* in 4.1 Demo A; the only change is the account boundary. Same-account, a direct resource grant stands alone; cross-account, the calling account must also grant `sts:AssumeRole` on the role. Add A's identity policy and it succeeds. The "both sides" rule is the whole headline.

*Checkpoint 1 logic:* remove the user from B's trust but keep A's identity grant → still fails. The trust policy is mandatory; an identity grant never substitutes for it. Same trust-policy-first exception from 4.1, now stretched across accounts.

**Demo B — confused deputy, `sts:ExternalId`.**
The scenario: B is your account, a SaaS vendor (account A) assumes a role in B to read your data, and the vendor uses **one principal to serve all its customers**. Without a guard, another of the vendor's customers could hand it your role ARN, and the vendor,  confused about whose behalf it's acting on would assume it. `ExternalId` is a shared secret placed in B's trust condition; only the legitimate integration sends it. Tested three ways: no id fails, wrong id fails, correct id works, with identity and trust both still allowing the call. ExternalId is not a second auth factor, it's a deputy-confusion gate, and it's specifically the **third-party/SaaS** case. It's the thing AWS tells vendors to require in your trust policy.

**Demo C — org data perimeter, `aws:PrincipalOrgID`.**
A and B share an org, so the trust condition uses `aws:PrincipalOrgID` instead of maintaining a list of account IDs. Caller in the org → works; a principal outside the org → denied (can't cleanly test without a third external account, but that denial is the point). `PrincipalOrgID` is the data-perimeter workhorse, and it lives far more often in **resource policies** (S3 bucket, KMS key) than in trust policies: "only principals in my org may touch this bucket."

---

## Which confused-deputy key, and when (the subtle one)

There are two flavors of confused deputy and they take different condition keys. Mixing them up is a classic wrong answer:

- **Third-party / cross-account principal** — a SaaS vendor's IAM principal assuming your role. Control: **`sts:ExternalId`**. The external party supplies the agreed secret; it defends against the vendor being tricked by another of its tenants.
- **AWS service principal** acting on your behalf — a service (CloudWatch, SNS, CloudTrail, etc.) assuming a role you handed it. Control: **`aws:SourceArn`** and/or **`aws:SourceAccount`** in the trust condition. Services don't pass an ExternalId; they identify themselves by the source resource and account, so ExternalId simply doesn't apply here.

Decision rule: `ExternalId` = human/third-party SaaS deputy; `aws:SourceArn` / `aws:SourceAccount` = AWS-service deputy. Reaching for ExternalId on a service-principal trust is the distractor.

---

## PrincipalOrgID — trust policy vs resource policy vs RCP

Same condition key, three places to put it, escalating in strength (this is the 4.3 → 4.4 connect-back, and Checkpoint 2):

- **In a trust policy** — scopes who can assume one role to the org. Per-role.
- **In a bucket/key resource policy** — restricts that one resource to org principals. Per-resource, and only as strong as that policy; whoever can edit the bucket policy can weaken it.
- **In an RCP (from 4.3)** — an org-wide cap on the resource side that an individual bucket policy **cannot override**. Centralized, applies across every in-scope resource and account, and can't be undone locally.

The intent difference: a bucket-policy condition is a local guard you trust each resource owner to keep; an RCP is an org guardrail no resource owner can remove. For "guarantee org-wide regardless of individual policies," the RCP is the more correct answer.

---

## Exam relevance

- **Cross-account assume fails even though the trust names the principal** → the calling account's identity policy is missing `sts:AssumeRole`. Both sides, every time.
- **Has the identity grant but the assume still fails** → trust doesn't name them, or a condition (`ExternalId` / `PrincipalOrgID`) isn't satisfied.
- **Third-party / SaaS assuming your role** → `ExternalId` is the confused-deputy answer; watch for it as the "most secure" option.
- **AWS service acting on your behalf** → `aws:SourceArn` / `aws:SourceAccount`, **not** ExternalId. The wrong-key distractor.
- **Org perimeter** → `aws:PrincipalOrgID`, mostly in resource policies; for centralized, un-overridable enforcement an RCP beats a per-resource policy.
- **Cross-account roles beat sharing access keys** across accounts — temporary, scoped, no long-lived secrets to leak (carryover from 4.1).
