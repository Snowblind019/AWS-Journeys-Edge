# 5.1 — KMS Key Policies vs IAM Policies

**Domain 5: Data Protection (18%)**
**Companion to:** `scs-c03-lab-5_1-kms-key-policies.md` (the runnable lab). This file records what was built and *why each result happens*, one level deeper than the lab's terse `Result:` lines.

**Cost:** about $0. A customer-managed key is $1/month, but billing stops the moment you schedule deletion, and the only residue is the 7-day minimum deletion window.

---

## What I built

- A **customer-managed KMS key** with an alias `alias/lab-dp-key`, whose key policy I rewrote three times to watch access flip.
- **`lab-kms-tester`**, a throwaway IAM user with its own profile, used as the principal making `kms:Encrypt` calls so the only thing changing between demos is the policy in play.
- Three key-policy shapes in sequence: an account-`:root` enabling statement, an admin-only policy with `:root` removed, and a policy naming the tester directly. Same encrypt call against each.

The point was to learn the one rule that makes KMS different from S3: the key policy is the primary access mechanism, and a same-account IAM allow does nothing unless the key policy permits it. This is the 4.1 trust-policy logic wearing a KMS hat.

---

## The evaluation model this proves

Every KMS key has exactly one resource-based policy, the **key policy** (always named `default`). For **same-account** access, the key policy is authoritative: an IAM identity policy can only grant KMS actions if the key policy first delegates that authority to IAM. The usual delegation is the account-`:root` statement granting `kms:*` to the account principal. Without it, IAM policies are powerless for that key and the key policy must name the principal directly.

| Key policy | IAM identity policy | Same-account result | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `:root` enabling statement | allows the KMS actions | Allowed | key policy delegates to IAM, both sides agree |
| `:root` enabling statement | absent or insufficient | Denied | IAM never granted the action |
| no enabling statement | allows the KMS actions | Denied | key policy doesn't enable IAM, so the IAM allow has no effect |
| names the principal directly | none | Allowed | the key policy is a direct resource-based grant, sufficient on its own |

This is the same split from 4.1, now for keys. A trust policy naming a principal directly is sufficient alone, and a trust policy delegating to `:root` hands the decision to IAM. A key policy works identically. KMS is *not* the plain same-account union you saw with S3 in 4.2, where an allow on either side is enough. With KMS, the key policy has to open the door before IAM can walk through it.

Cross-account is the exception that 5.2 picks up: there, both the key policy must allow the external principal **and** the caller's IAM policy must allow the action. Same intersection rule as the cross-account assume in 4.4.

## What each demo proved

**Demo A: `:root` delegates to IAM.**
The key policy gives the account principal (`:root`) full control, which enables IAM, and the tester's identity policy allows `Encrypt`, `Decrypt`, and `DescribeKey`. The encrypt succeeds because both sides said yes. The thing to internalize: `:root` grants the tester nothing by itself. It says "IAM policies in this account may control this key." Same shape as the account-root trust plus identity policy in 4.1 Demo B.

This demo also carries the single most-tested KMS misconception. `arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root` as a **principal** means the whole account 111122223333, not the root user. It delegates authority to IAM in that account. It does not grant any IAM user access on its own, and it does not mean "only the root user." If a question hinges on `:root` in a key policy, this is what it is testing.

**Demo B: remove the enabling statement and the IAM allow goes dead.**
Replace the key policy with one that names only your admin and drops `:root`. The tester's identity policy is **unchanged** and still allows `kms:Encrypt`, yet the same encrypt now fails with `AccessDeniedException` ("no resource-based policy allows the kms:Encrypt action"). The IAM allow had no effect because the key policy stopped enabling IAM for that principal. That is the whole KMS rule in one result: for a same-account allow, an IAM grant is never sufficient on its own.

**Demo C: direct grant in the key policy, no IAM needed.**
Detach the tester's IAM policy entirely so it has zero KMS permissions in IAM, then name the tester directly in the key policy. The encrypt succeeds. The key policy alone is sufficient, exactly like naming a principal directly in a role's trust policy in 4.1 Demo A. This is why same-account key access can be controlled by the key policy alone, and it sets up the cross-account contrast in 5.2.

## The Deny asymmetry and the lockout trap (the subtle one)

**Allow needs the key policy. Deny does not.**
You proved that a same-account *allow* requires the key policy's permission. An explicit *deny* is not symmetric. A `Deny` on `kms:Decrypt` in an IAM policy or an SCP blocks the caller regardless of what the key policy says, because an explicit deny anywhere always wins. The practical consequence: a permissions boundary or SCP that denies KMS actions is an effective guardrail even on keys whose policies you don't control. The key policy gates allow, but it cannot rescue a principal from an explicit deny somewhere else.

**Lockout is real and unrecoverable.**
A KMS key has exactly one policy, always named `default`. If you write a key policy that names no principal you control, the key becomes permanently unmanageable, and AWS Support cannot fix it for you. That is why every policy in the lab keeps an `AdminOnly` statement pointed at a real admin ARN. Treat "always retain a controllable admin statement" as a hard rule, not a nicety.

**Operational note on deletion.**
You cannot hard-delete a key immediately. The pending window is 7 to 30 days, billing stops once deletion is scheduled, and you can reverse it with `cancel-key-deletion` during the window.

---

## Exam relevance

- **`:root` in a key policy means the account delegating to IAM**, not the root user, and not a standalone grant to anyone. The most-tested KMS misconception.
- **A same-account KMS allow needs the key policy to permit it.** An IAM allow alone is not sufficient unless the key policy enables IAM (the `:root` statement) or names the principal directly.
- **A key policy naming a principal directly grants access with no IAM policy**, the same as a trust policy naming a principal.
- **Cross-account KMS needs both sides:** the key policy allows the external principal and the caller's IAM policy allows the action. That is the focus of 5.2.
- **Explicit deny in IAM, an SCP, or a boundary beats any key-policy allow.** The deny asymmetry is what makes an SCP or boundary an effective KMS guardrail.
- **Lockout:** one policy named `default`, always keep a controllable admin statement, and remember Support cannot recover a locked-out key.
