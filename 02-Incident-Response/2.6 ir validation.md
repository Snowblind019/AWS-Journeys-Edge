## What I worked through

- Framed the skill: an IR plan you have never exercised is a **hypothesis**, so validation means turning it into evidence by injecting controlled faults and events without the test becoming the incident.
- Built the **wrong answers first**: treating a documented runbook as validated, running FIS in prod with no stop conditions, and using Resilience Hub to inject faults, and saw why each fails.

The point was that validation is demonstrated, not documented. The two axes are technical and human, CloudWatch alarm stop conditions are the mandatory guardrail on both, and the named-tool trio is exactly what the exam swaps.

## The model this proves

An IR plan you have never exercised is a hypothesis, not a capability, and this skill turns it into evidence. Validation runs on two axes with one shared guardrail.

| Axis | Tools | Validates |
|---|---|---|
| Technical | FIS (faults), GuardDuty sample findings (detection) | detection fires, automation contains |
| Human | GameDay (runbook rehearsal), ARC (recovery) | responders and failover work |
| Guardrail (both) | CloudWatch alarm stop conditions | halt before the test becomes the incident |

FIS is the centerpiece, running real actions on real resources, so its template has three parts you memorize:

| Part | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `actions` | what fault to inject |
| `targets` | which resources, tag or percentage scoped to bound the blast radius |
| `stopConditions` | a CloudWatch alarm that auto-halts the experiment |

And the three named tools are constantly confused:

| Tool | Role | Verb it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Resilience Hub | assesses posture, finds gaps | "identify resilience gaps" |
| FIS | executes the experiment on real resources | "inject a fault, run an experiment" |
| ARC | proves recovery (readiness, routing controls, zonal shift) | "fail over, prove recovery" |

The whole thing is the chaos-engineering loop: steady state, hypothesis, inject, observe, improve.

## What each demo proved

**Wrong answer A: a documented runbook is not validation.**
A runbook in a wiki is paper, and the first time anyone runs it is during the real fire, which is when the gaps surface: the role never pre-staged, the alarm that never fired, the Lambda permission too narrow. Documentation is not validation. You validate by exercising the plan (GameDay) and instrumenting it (FIS) against measurable success criteria.

**Wrong answer B: FIS in prod with no stop conditions.**
Without a stop condition nothing halts the experiment when it starts cascading, so the test becomes a sev-1. Stop conditions are CloudWatch alarms that automatically halt the experiment when a threshold is breached, and they are the mandatory guardrail. You combine them with tag or percentage targets to bound the blast radius and run in pre-production first. This is the highest-yield FIS fact.

**Wrong answer C: Resilience Hub to inject faults.**
Resilience Hub assesses resilience posture and identifies gaps, it does not execute fault injection. Resilience Hub finds the gaps, FIS runs the experiment that proves whether the design holds, and the exam swaps them. If the verb is inject a fault or run an experiment, it is FIS.

**The correct workflow.**
Define a steady state and a hypothesis, validate the detection pipeline with GuardDuty sample findings (confirming the EventBridge rule fired, the runbook isolated, and the finding moved to RESOLVED, the 2.4 and 2.5 chain), validate the fault side with FIS using scoped targets and stop conditions in pre-prod first, wrap it in a GameDay that hands responders only the alert and measures against success criteria like contained in 15 minutes, orchestrate multi-step flows with Step Functions, and prove recovery with ARC readiness checks and a routing control or zonal shift. Then improve and repeat on a cadence.

## The subtle one: demonstrated not documented, stop conditions, and the tool trio

Several tested edges here.

**Validation is demonstrated, not documented.** A peer-reviewed runbook is a hypothesis, and "we have never had an incident so it must work" is survivorship, not evidence. You need instrumented experiments, the detection-pipeline check, human rehearsal, and measurable criteria.

**Stop conditions are the mandatory FIS guardrail.** They are CloudWatch alarms that auto-halt the experiment, and combined with tag or percentage targets and pre-prod-first they bound the blast radius. "No stop conditions for realism" is always wrong, because controlled and scoped beats dramatic.

**The Resilience Hub, FIS, ARC trio.** Resilience Hub assesses and finds gaps, FIS executes and runs the experiment, and ARC recovers with readiness checks, routing controls, and zonal shift. Resilience Hub versus FIS is the swap to watch, and ARC is the recover-phase tool that proves failover, not just containment.

**FIS and sample findings are both needed.** FIS breaks infrastructure, and sample findings exercise the detection-to-remediation pipeline. Validating one is not validating the other.

## Exam relevance

- **Validation is demonstrated, not documented.** The correct answer names FIS experiments, GuardDuty sample findings, GameDays, and measurable success criteria on a cadence. A runbook or "never had an incident" is wrong.
- **FIS runs real actions on real resources, and stop conditions (CloudWatch alarms that auto-halt) are the non-negotiable guardrail**, with tag or percentage targets and pre-prod first. Maximizing blast radius for realism is always wrong.
- **Keep the trio straight:** Resilience Hub assesses and finds gaps, FIS executes the experiment, ARC proves recovery (readiness checks, routing controls, zonal shift). Resilience Hub versus FIS is the swap.
- **FIS breaks infrastructure and sample findings exercise the detection pipeline.** You need both.
- **The FIS template is `actions`, `targets` (scoped), and `stopConditions`.**
- **Step Functions orchestrate multi-step validation and response**, and the loop is steady state, hypothesis, inject, observe, improve.
