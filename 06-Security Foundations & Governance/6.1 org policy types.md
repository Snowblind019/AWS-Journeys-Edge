# 6.1 — Organizations Policy Types Beyond SCPs

**Domain 6: Security Foundations and Governance (14%)**
**Companion to:** `scs-c03-lab-6_1-org-policy-types.md` (the runnable lab). This file records what was built and *why each result happens*, one level deeper than the lab's terse `Result:` lines.

**Cost:** $0. Enabling policy types costs nothing, and the lab is Organizations, IAM, and one S3 bucket. The only caution is operational: detach every restrictive policy at teardown, or a leftover Deny RCP keeps breaking access.

---

## What I built

- Enabled the three newer Organizations policy types at the root (RCP, declarative for EC2, AI opt-out) and saw each silently attach a `FullAWSAccess`-style default.
- Broke the wrong mental model that a resource policy is the final word, by attaching an **RCP that denied my own GET** even though I owned the bucket and had admin, then relaxed it to an **org-perimeter** form.
- Proved **why an SCP cannot do that job**, since an SCP never sees an external principal a bucket policy grants access to.
- Attached a **declarative EC2 policy** (block public AMI sharing) and an **AI services opt-out**, two mechanisms not to confuse with RCP.

The point was that Domain 6 is the org-wide policy toolbox beyond SCPs, and the whole subdomain is telling four mechanisms apart. RCP is the resource-side deny-list that closes the external-principal gap SCPs cannot, declarative policies enforce a durable config baseline rather than denying an API, and AI opt-out governs content use, not access.

---

## The model this proves

Organizations has policy types beyond SCPs, each doing a distinct job, and they share one property with SCPs: enabling a type silently attaches a `FullAWSAccess`-equivalent default, so they are deny-lists layered over an implicit full-allow and never grant.

| Policy type | What it controls | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| SCP | max permissions of your principals | principal-side deny-list |
| RCP | max permissions on your resources, any principal | resource-side deny-list |
| Declarative (EC2) | a configuration baseline, the desired end state | durable config enforcement |
| AI opt-out | whether AWS uses your content to improve AI services | content governance |

The heart of the subdomain is the SCP-versus-RCP direction of control:

| Threat | Control | Because |
|---|---|---|
| my identity reaching an untrusted resource or network | SCP (principal-side) | caps what my principals can do |
| an untrusted identity reaching my resource | RCP (resource-side) | evaluated on the resource regardless of principal |
| an external account granted via a bucket policy | RCP | an SCP never sees external principals |

---

## What each demo proved

**Enabling a type attaches an allow-everything default.**
The moment you enable RCPs, AWS attaches `RCPFullAWSAccess` to every entity, an allow-everything pass-through so the feature does not lock anyone out. Your restriction only takes effect when you attach a Deny-style RCP on top. RCPs and SCPs are deny-lists over an implicit full-allow, and they never grant.

**RCP as an upper bound: it denies your own GET.**
With an RCP that denies `s3:GetObject` attached to the member account, the GET fails even though your identity policy is full S3, you own the bucket, and no bucket policy denies you. The RCP is an upper bound on the resource side, and a Deny there wins the intersection, so an identity-based admin policy cannot override it. This is the same evaluation logic as the SCP proof in 4.3 and the KMS intersection in 5.2, one layer out.

**RCP as a data perimeter.**
Swapping to a perimeter RCP that denies `s3:*` unless `aws:PrincipalOrgID` matches your org lets your own GET work again, because you are in the org, while the bucket is now reachable only by identities in your org regardless of what the bucket policy says.

**Why an SCP cannot do it.**
An SCP caps principals in your member accounts, so if an admin rewrites the bucket policy to grant `s3:GetObject` to an external account's root, the SCP never sees that external principal and the external account reads your objects. An RCP is evaluated whenever the resource is accessed regardless of who the principal is, so the external GET is denied because `aws:PrincipalOrgID` does not match, even though the bucket policy allows it. That is the structural gap RCPs close.

**Declarative policy: a durable config baseline.**
A declarative EC2 policy that blocks public AMI sharing enforces a configuration baseline, not a deny on a specific API, so it stays enforced even if AWS adds a new share pathway later, because you declared the desired end state.

**AI opt-out: content governance, not access.**
An AI services opt-out policy opts your org out of AWS using your content to develop or improve covered AI services, and it does not block anyone from using those services and is not Bedrock guardrails.

## The subtle one: direction of control, the SLR exception, declarative versus SCP, and what AI opt-out does not do

Several tested edges here.

**Direction of control is the whole question.** Stop my identities from reaching untrusted resources or networks is an SCP, principal-side. Stop untrusted identities from reaching my resources is an RCP, resource-side. Both together are the full data perimeter, and if the threat actor is outside your org, an SCP is almost always the wrong answer.

**The service-linked-role exception is the top RCP outage cause.** RCPs do not apply to service-linked roles, and many AWS services reach your resources via SLRs or service principals (S3 logging, replication), so a naive org-perimeter RCP that omits the `aws:PrincipalIsAWSService` or `aws:PrincipalServiceName` exceptions breaks legitimate AWS service access.

**Declarative versus SCP.** A durable config baseline that persists even as the service evolves is a declarative policy, while an SCP denying a specific action is brittle because a future API can bypass it. The keyword tells are configuration, baseline, and persists or maintained even when new features are added.

**AI opt-out does not block usage.** It only governs whether AWS may retain or use your content to improve the service. Preventing invocation of an AI model is IAM or an SCP, not an opt-out policy, and this is not Bedrock guardrails.

## Exam relevance

- **Enabling any Organizations policy type attaches a `FullAWSAccess`-style default**, so RCPs and SCPs are deny-lists over an implicit allow and never grant.
- **An RCP is a resource-side upper bound**, and a Deny wins the intersection even against an identity admin who owns the resource. Same logic as SCPs and KMS key policies.
- **SCP versus RCP is direction of control:** SCP caps your principals (stop my identities reaching untrusted resources), RCP caps access to your resources regardless of principal (stop untrusted identities reaching my resources). An external threat, including a bucket policy granting an external account, needs an RCP.
- **Org-perimeter RCPs must except AWS service principals** (`aws:PrincipalIsAWSService`, `aws:PrincipalServiceName`) or they break service-linked-role access. This is the top RCP outage cause.
- **Declarative policies enforce a durable configuration baseline that survives new APIs**, unlike a brittle SCP action-deny. Keywords: configuration, baseline, persists.
- **AI opt-out policies govern whether AWS uses your content to improve AI services**, and do not block usage or equal Bedrock guardrails.
