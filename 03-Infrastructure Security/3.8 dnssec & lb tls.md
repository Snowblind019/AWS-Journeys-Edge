## What I built

- **DNSSEC signing** on a hosted zone: created an asymmetric ECC_NIST_P256 KMS key in us-east-1 for the KSK, enabled zone signing, and read the DS record to add at the parent to complete the chain of trust.
- **Load balancer TLS posture:** set an HTTPS listener security policy to a TLS 1.3 capable policy, then configured ALB mutual TLS in verify mode against an uploaded trust store, and contrasted it with passthrough and with NLB's lack of managed mTLS.

The point was two independent hardening topics. DNSSEC protects the integrity of DNS answers and has a nasty KMS-linked outage mode, and the load balancer's TLS posture controls both what is negotiated (security policy) and whether the client is authenticated (mTLS), where ALB verify is the only LB-managed client authentication.

## The model this proves

**Part A, DNSSEC.** Plaintext DNS answers can be spoofed or cache-poisoned by an attacker positioned between a client and its resolver, swapping a real IP for a malicious one to redirect users to a phishing site. DNSSEC cryptographically signs records so resolvers can confirm they were not tampered with. There are two features, and the exam checks which side you are on:

| Feature | Side | What it protects | Who runs it |
|---|---|---|---|
| DNSSEC signing | authoritative, your zone | others' resolvers can verify your records | you sign the hosted zone |
| DNSSEC validation | resolver, your VPC | your workloads reject forged answers | the Route 53 Resolver validates |

Signing uses two keys, and the chain of trust is completed only when you add a DS record (a digest of the KSK public key) at the parent zone or registrar:

| Key | Managed by | Backing |
|---|---|---|
| ZSK (zone signing key) | Route 53, auto-rotated | internal |
| KSK (key signing key) | you | asymmetric customer-managed KMS key, ECC_NIST_P256, us-east-1 |

**Part B, load balancer TLS posture.** Domain 5 put the certificate on the listener. This is about what the listener negotiates and whether it authenticates the client, which are two separate knobs:

- The **security policy** selects allowed TLS versions and cipher suites. Set a TLS 1.2 minimum at least, and prefer a TLS 1.3 capable policy. FIPS and forward-secrecy policies exist for compliance.
- **Mutual TLS** makes the client present a certificate too, and only the ALB can do LB-managed validation:

| Mode | Who validates the client cert | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| off | nobody, no client cert required | default |
| verify (ALB) | the ALB, against a trust store | rejects untrusted, expired, or revoked certs at the LB |
| passthrough (ALB) | the backend | forwards the chain in `X-Amzn-Mtls-Clientcert` |
| NLB | the targets only | NLB has no LB-managed mTLS, TCP passthrough only |

## What each part proved

**DNSSEC: signing is not protection until the DS record lands.**
Creating the KSK (backed by the asymmetric ECC_NIST_P256 KMS key in us-east-1) and enabling signing signs the zone, but validators see nothing until you add the DS record at the parent. Signing alone is a signed-but-unprotected zone.

**Load balancer security policy: what gets negotiated.**
The listener's security policy is where you set the TLS floor. A TLS 1.3 capable policy such as `ELBSecurityPolicy-TLS13-1-2-2021-06` with a TLS 1.2 minimum is the baseline, and FIPS or forward-secrecy variants cover compliance needs.

**ALB mTLS: verify versus passthrough.**
In verify mode the ALB authenticates the client certificate against an uploaded trust store (a CA bundle, optionally with CRLs) and rejects untrusted, expired, or revoked certificates before traffic reaches the app, offloading client authentication from the backend. In passthrough mode the ALB does not validate, it forwards the whole client certificate chain to the target in the `X-Amzn-Mtls-Clientcert` header and the backend validates.

**NLB: TLS termination but no managed mTLS.**
An NLB can terminate TLS to centralize certificates in ACM and offload the handshake, but it does not support LB-managed mTLS. If you need mutual TLS on an NLB, you use TCP passthrough and let the targets validate the client certificates.

## The subtle one: the DNSSEC outage modes, and ALB-only mTLS verify

Two areas carry the exam weight here.

**DNSSEC's real risk is operational and lives on the KMS side.** If the customer-managed key behind the KSK loses permissions or is deleted, the KSK moves to `ACTION_NEEDED` and validating resolvers eventually return `SERVFAIL`, which takes the domain offline for everyone using DNSSEC validation. Alarm on `DNSSECInternalFailure` and `DNSSECKeySigningKeysNeedingAction`. Disabling DNSSEC in the wrong order causes the same `SERVFAIL` outage, so remove the DS record at the parent first, wait out its TTL, and only then disable signing. While signing is on, Route 53 caps record TTLs at one week, and KSK rotation is your responsibility while Route 53 handles the ZSK.

**mTLS verify is an ALB capability only.** If a question asks for the load balancer itself to authenticate client certificates, the answer is ALB verify mode, not NLB, which can only pass certificates through to the targets. Tightening the security policy (dropping TLS 1.0 and 1.1) can also break older clients, so watch `ClientTLSNegotiationErrorCount` before and after the change, since a spike means some clients can no longer connect.

---

## Exam relevance

- **DNSSEC signing is the authoritative side** (protect your zone for others' resolvers), and **DNSSEC validation is the resolver side** (protect your workloads from forged answers). Know which side the question is on.
- **The KSK is backed by an asymmetric ECC_NIST_P256 customer-managed KMS key in us-east-1**, and the ZSK is Route 53's to rotate. Complete the chain of trust with a DS record at the parent.
- **DNSSEC outages are KMS-linked:** key loss moves the KSK to `ACTION_NEEDED` and yields `SERVFAIL`. Disable by removing the DS record first, then signing, and alarm on `DNSSECInternalFailure` and `DNSSECKeySigningKeysNeedingAction`.
- **The listener security policy sets TLS versions and ciphers**, so prefer TLS 1.3 capable with a TLS 1.2 floor. Tightening it can break old clients, watched via `ClientTLSNegotiationErrorCount`.
- **ALB mTLS verify authenticates client certs at the load balancer** against a trust store, while passthrough forwards the chain in `X-Amzn-Mtls-Clientcert` for the backend to validate.
- **NLB has no LB-managed mTLS.** LB-authenticated client certificates means ALB verify, not NLB.
